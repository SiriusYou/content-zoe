# You Cannot Govern Healthcare AI You Cannot Find

<!-- EVIDENCE_GRADE_WARN: This report is limited to staged source material on AI inventory, health IT safety, decision support, risk management, governance, device context, and professional guidance. It does not include local inventories, hidden-use audits, procurement records, utilization logs, legal analysis, measured outcomes, or named implementation proof. -->

Healthcare AI governance often begins with downstream questions: Is a tool approved? Is it being monitored? Is it safe to keep using? Is it ready to retire? The staged sources support a more basic question beneath all of those: what AI-enabled workflows exist locally in the first place? If that list is missing, later governance steps have no stable object to review.

This report makes an inventory argument, not a market argument. It does not claim that any named hospital, health system, vendor, product, or public program has a complete or incomplete inventory. It does not prove hidden local AI use, mature oversight, or measured outcomes. Its narrower claim is that governance needs a local, reviewable register before it can reliably review, monitor, update, narrow, pause, replace, or retire AI-enabled workflows.

## Why Governance Breaks Without the List

- A workflow that is not listed cannot be revisited on schedule.
- A workflow with no recorded owner cannot be assigned accountable oversight.
- A workflow that is absent from the register cannot easily be linked to a near miss, a change record, a renewal discussion, or a retirement decision.

That is why inventory is not clerical overhead. NIST AI RMF supports governance, mapping, roles, measurement, management, and lifecycle review. ONC SAFER materials reinforce the operational need to understand systems, interfaces, maintenance, contingency planning, and change. WHO, AMA, and Joint Commission with CHAI materials support the same point from oversight, accountability, workflow fit, and responsible-adoption perspectives.

The evidence still has sharp limits. No staged source provides a local inventory, a hidden-use audit, a procurement log, a utilization log, a user interview set, or measured outcome data. The strongest source-bounded conclusion is therefore not that the right list already exists, but that governance becomes fragile when the list does not exist in a reviewable local form.

## What a Reviewable Register Should Contain

A usable register needs more than a product name. The staged sources support a local record that identifies at least:

- Workflow or operational use case
- Accountable owner or owner group
- Intended users or user group
- Care or operational setting
- Patient or staff pathway touched
- Operational status
- Review date or review thread

Status should be operational rather than decorative. The staged sources support categories such as proposed, piloted, active, narrowed, paused, retired, replaced, or pending review.

The register becomes more useful when it also links each item to approved use, source material, evidence, monitoring, change records, near-miss records, a fallback path, renewal review, and retirement state when those records exist. Unknown fields should remain visible rather than being filled with assumptions.

That warning is central to the thesis. A register is a governance surface, not a proof artifact. It can help reviewers ask what exists, what changed, who owns it, and when it should return for review. By itself, it does not establish that a listed workflow is safe, effective, fair, compliant, trusted, or operationally mature.

## The Inventory Has to Reach Embedded and Indirect AI

The inventory argument fails if it covers only tools that are easy to name in procurement conversations. The staged sources support looking for embedded and indirect AI across EHR modules, decision support, documentation, patient portals, imaging support, coding and billing, scheduling, triage, referral routing, prior-authorization support, operations queues, analytics, and quality reporting.

That broader scope matters because AI-enabled functions can sit inside ordinary workflow systems rather than in standalone products labeled as AI. A governance team that asks only for a vendor list may miss AI-enabled functions already embedded in familiar platforms. The relevant question is not whether every automation is AI. It is whether an AI-enabled workflow influences care, documentation, routing, operations, communication, or administration in a way that should be listed and revisited.

The staged sources also support surfacing informal or indirect use for review, but they do not support claiming that any specific hidden tool exists locally.

## Ownership, Use Boundaries, and Review Routing

An inventory becomes operational only when each item has accountable ownership and a defined use boundary. The staged sources support recording who owns the workflow, who is expected to use it, what setting it belongs to, what pathway it touches, what use is approved, what use remains unreviewed or out of scope, and where concerns should escalate.

Risk routing belongs here too, but in a bounded form. The sources support using impact questions to determine review depth, including whether a workflow influences clinical judgment, affects patient-facing action, relies on sensitive data, creates strong user dependence, changes frequently, or lacks a workable fallback path. They do not support presenting a universal high-medium-low scheme as settled doctrine. Risk tiers can help route attention; they do not prove safety or maturity.

Ownership also requires decision rights. Someone should be able to continue, narrow, pause, retire, or reopen review for the workflow. Without that accountability, the register remains descriptive when governance needs it to be actionable.

## Source Attributes and Workflow Context Strengthen Reviewability

The register is stronger when it links not just to a workflow name but to the context around that workflow. ONC decision-support material supports attention to source attributes, references, logic visibility, and implementation information where available. NIST supports context mapping, documentation, measurement, and monitoring.

That makes it reasonable to include fields such as data source, integration point, output type, expected human action, downstream workflow, monitoring signal, and fallback path when those fields fit the use case. These links help reviewers understand how an AI-enabled workflow reaches practice and help separate vendor evidence from local evidence.

FDA AI- or ML-enabled medical device material can contribute bounded device-context evidence for some identifiable functions. It should remain bounded. It does not define the full healthcare AI landscape, and it should not substitute for a broader local inventory that also covers administrative, operational, documentation, communication, and decision-support workflows outside that frame.

## A Register Has to Stay Alive

An inventory that is never revisited becomes stale documentation. The staged sources support periodic review, update triggers, owner confirmation, and explicit retired-state handling. Review should ask whether the workflow is still active, still owned, still monitored, still in scope, and still tied to current evidence and current workflow reality.

That lifecycle logic still matters after active use ends. A retired or replaced workflow may leave behind dependencies, stored data, contractual questions, patient communication issues, or fallback obligations. The staged sources do not support legal conclusions about those obligations, but they do support the operational claim that retirement should not mean erasing the record.

The same logic applies to change history and near misses. If a workflow changes, narrows, pauses, or returns to review, the register should show that movement. If a near miss or incident informs review, the inventory should be able to link the item to that thread. Otherwise governance loses the continuity needed to explain why a workflow remained active, changed scope, or left use.

## What This Report Can and Cannot Establish

The staged sources support a practical conclusion: before healthcare AI governance can review or monitor local use, it needs a local, reviewable inventory of AI-enabled workflows. They also support a more specific conclusion: the inventory should be operational rather than ceremonial, and it should include embedded uses, ownership, use boundaries, evidence links, review dates, and retirement state where applicable.

The staged sources do not establish whether any specific organization has already done this well. They do not establish completeness, measured benefit, safety, fairness, compliance, trust, or maturity. They do not establish the existence of hidden tools in any particular setting. They provide the questions a local inventory should answer, not the local facts that only an organization can supply.
