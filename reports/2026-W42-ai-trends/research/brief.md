# Research Brief: Healthcare AI Needs Usable Transparency

## Scope

This brief is limited to staged local source material about healthcare AI governance and transparency. It does not support a news roundup, vendor comparison, product ranking, deployment case study, or outcome claim.

## Core Thesis

Healthcare AI transparency is becoming an operating requirement, not a disclosure slogan. The report should test whether the people around an AI-enabled workflow can see enough to understand intended use, source attributes, evidence limits, local fit, monitoring needs, and escalation paths.

## Implementation Direction

1. Lead with a short evidence-grade warning before the H1.
2. Keep the body organized around workflow usability, not abstract policy.
3. Treat source attributes, public device lists, and assurance records as question-raising artifacts, not proof artifacts.
4. End on local governance responsibility: national frameworks help define questions, but local organizations still own fit, oversight, and monitoring.

## Safe Claims To Carry Forward

- Transparency is more useful when it explains intended use, intended users, workflow context, inputs and outputs, known limitations, monitoring status, and escalation paths.
- Transparency needs differ by audience; clinicians, patients, administrators, procurement, and governance teams do not need identical detail.
- ONC-style source attributes and predictive DSI risk records provide a useful vocabulary for asking what a tool is, what evidence it relies on, and what controls exist in covered contexts.
- FDA-style public AI-device lists can improve visibility, but they are not comprehensive and should be treated as starting points for review.
- Assurance is stronger when it includes local workflow review, local data and population fit, accountable ownership, and post-deployment monitoring.
- Lifecycle transparency matters because tools, data, interfaces, and workflows can change over time.

## Claims To Avoid

- Do not claim transparency proves safety, effectiveness, fairness, trustworthiness, privacy safety, or clinical benefit.
- Do not imply every healthcare AI tool is an FDA-regulated device or an ONC-covered predictive DSI.
- Do not claim any local validation, deployment success, or named organization performance.
- Do not convert a public summary, checklist, source-attribute record, or change record into proof of real-world performance.
- Do not write this as a weekly market roundup unless new staged evidence is added.

## Suggested Section Flow

1. Why transparency has to work inside the workflow, not just on paper.
2. What usable transparency looks like for different audiences.
3. How source attributes and predictive DSI records make some tools more inspectable.
4. Why public AI-device lists help visibility but remain incomplete.
5. Why local assurance, monitoring, and change records are still necessary after launch.
