# Communication and recourse after events

## Editorial use

Use this file to connect incident response with communication and recourse without making legal claims. The report should say that when an AI-enabled workflow creates a safety concern or near miss, people need a way to understand what happened, correct the path, and know who owns follow-up.

## Source basis

- AHRQ CANDOR and patient-safety response materials support communication, event investigation, analysis, and resolution as safety practices.
- WHO health-AI ethics material supports transparency, accountability, human oversight, and protection of affected people.
- AMA augmented-intelligence material supports professional responsibility, patient relationship, workflow, training, privacy, and accountability.

## Reader-safe claims

- Communication needs depend on the event, the patient-facing impact, and local policy; the report should not define legal duties.
- Staff need a route to report concerns without forcing them to decide alone whether an event is legally reportable.
- Patients and caregivers may need a way to ask questions, correct information, or understand a changed path when an AI-enabled workflow affected their care experience.
- Recourse should identify who can review, override, correct, pause, or route around the workflow.
- Follow-up should tell internal stakeholders what changed after the event, not only that the report was received.

## Claims to avoid

- Do not give disclosure, apology, privilege, malpractice, PSQIA, HIPAA, or professional-liability advice.
- Do not claim any patient was owed a specific communication.
- Do not claim communication proves trust, transparency, or safety culture.
- Do not imply patient-facing communication is always the same for near misses and harms.

## Evidence limit

The staged sources support communication and recourse questions. They do not include local patient communications, disclosure decisions, complaint files, legal analysis, or implementation evidence.
