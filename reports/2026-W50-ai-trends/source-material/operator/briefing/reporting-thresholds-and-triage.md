# Reporting thresholds and triage

## Editorial use

Use this file to explain why reporting rules and triage routes need to be explicit. The report should show that vague "tell someone if AI acts strangely" guidance is not enough for a clinical workflow.

## Source basis

- AHRQ event-reporting and response materials support structured event reporting, investigation, analysis, and follow-up.
- ONC SAFER contingency planning supports explicit planning for system interruptions, downtime, and fallback operations.
- NIST AI RMF supports risk response tied to severity, context, and lifecycle monitoring.

## Reader-safe claims

- A reporting threshold should tell staff what to capture, where to send it, and how urgent the response should be.
- Triage should separate immediate safety concerns from lower-urgency evidence gaps without losing either category.
- The triage record should ask whether the workflow should continue, narrow, pause, route to fallback, notify a vendor, reopen validation, or escalate to patient-safety, clinical, IT, quality, compliance, or operations owners.
- Downtime and fallback events deserve attention because a safe model can still sit inside an unsafe interruption or recovery path.
- Timing matters: the record should capture when the issue was observed, when it was escalated, and when follow-up occurred.

## Claims to avoid

- Do not claim the staged sources define a universal threshold for all AI incidents.
- Do not give legal advice about mandatory reporting, disclosure, malpractice, FDA, HIPAA, or PSO handling.
- Do not claim triage prevents harm.
- Do not invent severity classes or local escalation policies.

## Evidence limit

The staged sources support triage and response questions. They do not include a local policy, severity matrix, legal determination, vendor contract, event report, or follow-up record.
