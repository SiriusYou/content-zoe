## Facts and observations

- The FDA maintains an AI-enabled medical device list as a transparency resource for devices authorized for marketing in the United States. The list is useful as a directional signal for regulated device activity, but it should not be treated as a complete census of every AI tool deployed in care delivery.
- The FDA's AI software-as-a-medical-device page frames AI/ML device regulation around established premarket pathways such as 510(k), De Novo, and PMA, plus review of significant software modifications when patient risk warrants it.
- FDA material continues to emphasize total-product-lifecycle oversight for AI-enabled device software. This matters because adaptive or frequently updated models create postmarket change-management questions that traditional static-device review was not built around.
- FDA's PCCP guidance is directly relevant to iterative AI device improvement: a predetermined change control plan describes planned modifications, the methodology for developing, validating, and implementing those modifications, and the impact assessment for those modifications.
- The international PCCP principles from FDA, Health Canada, and MHRA emphasize focused and bounded changes, risk-based planning, evidence generation, transparency, and total-product-lifecycle monitoring.

## W26 framing

- Reader-safe headline: regulatory activity is moving from "AI exists in medical devices" toward change-management and transparency infrastructure for AI-enabled devices.
- Do not overclaim that FDA authorization proves broad clinical adoption. It proves that specific devices met a marketing pathway, not that every health system uses them.
- Evidence limit: these FDA sources are strong for regulatory posture and device oversight. They are weak for provider adoption, operational deployment, payer use, or patient outcomes.
