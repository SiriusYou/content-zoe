# Patient feedback and recourse

## Editorial use

Use this file to keep the report from treating transparency as a one-way notice. Trust improves when patients and clinicians have routes to ask questions, correct context, challenge decisions, report concerns, and trigger review. The report should frame feedback and recourse as governance infrastructure.

## Source basis to cite cautiously

- WHO ethics and governance guidance supports accountability, responsiveness, human oversight, and respect for people affected by health AI.
- AMA augmented-intelligence materials support physician responsibility and AI as assistive rather than replacement decision-making.
- AHRQ patient-safety and learning-health-system concepts support feedback, event review, near-miss learning, and improvement routines.
- Joint Commission responsible health-data materials support patient transparency and governance around data use.

## Supported claims

- Patient and clinician feedback routes help organizations notice when AI-mediated workflows are confusing, wrong, unfair, or hard to challenge.
- Recourse should identify who reviews concerns, what evidence is examined, and what actions can follow, such as workflow clarification, retraining, access restriction, vendor inquiry, or tool pause.
- Feedback is more credible when connected to decision records and learning loops rather than handled as isolated complaints.

## Unsafe claims

- Do not claim a feedback process prevents harm.
- Do not imply every AI-mediated decision requires a separate appeal process.
- Do not say patients used or benefited from recourse without staged evidence.

## Evidence warning

The staged sources support feedback and recourse as trust mechanisms. They do not measure whether patients use them or whether they improve outcomes.
