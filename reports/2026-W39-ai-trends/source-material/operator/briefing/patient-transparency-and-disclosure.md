# Patient transparency and disclosure

## Editorial use

Use this file to frame patient-facing transparency as an operating duty. The report can say that healthcare AI governance is becoming more credible when organizations can explain when AI or AI-adjacent data use matters to care, administration, or secondary data use. The safe emphasis is disclosure, context, and accountability routes, not proof that patients understand or accept the practice.

## Source basis to cite cautiously

- AMA augmented-intelligence principles and disclosure materials support transparency, physician responsibility, and careful communication about AI-enabled systems.
- ONC/ASTP HTI-1 decision-support intervention materials support source attributes and transparency for predictive tools in certified health IT contexts.
- WHO ethics and governance guidance supports transparency, explainability, accountability, and respect for people affected by health AI.
- Joint Commission Responsible Use of Health Data materials support patient transparency around secondary use of de-identified health data.

## Supported claims

- Patient-facing trust depends partly on whether organizations can explain what AI is doing, what it is not doing, and who remains accountable.
- Disclosure should be connected to meaningful context: intended use, source limitations, whether recommendations are assistive, and how concerns can be escalated.
- Transparency is stronger when it reaches both clinicians and patients rather than remaining only in internal governance documents.

## Unsafe claims

- Do not equate disclosure with informed consent.
- Do not claim patients understand or trust a system because disclosure materials exist.
- Do not imply transparency resolves bias, privacy, safety, or performance risk.

## Evidence warning

The staged sources support transparency and disclosure as governance duties. They do not measure patient comprehension, consent quality, or trust outcomes.
