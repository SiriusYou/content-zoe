# Research Brief

## Working thesis

Healthcare AI is moving from adoption interest and governance language toward operational proof. The strongest supported angle is that responsible use now depends on what organizations can inspect, monitor, document, and learn from after deployment.

## Supported narrative

- Reviewability matters: transparency fields, decision-support source attributes, interoperability, and exchange infrastructure can make AI systems more inspectable, but they do not prove accuracy, fairness, safety, or local readiness.
- Assurance is operating work: evidence review, risk assessment, governance routines, accountable ownership, and safety learning are recurring requirements across the staged sources.
- Change discipline matters: FDA device-software materials offer a bounded example of planned updates, modification protocols, performance evaluation, and postmarket monitoring.
- Vendor management is part of assurance: supply-chain transparency, contract terms, data-flow visibility, cybersecurity resilience, testing claims, monitoring duties, and incident response shape healthcare AI risk.
- Human accountability and privacy remain hard boundaries: physician exposure increases pressure for training, escalation, review, and documentation, while privacy and ethics references define guardrails rather than proof of deployment readiness.

## Safe claims

- Healthcare AI assurance needs evidence review, monitoring, safety learning, accountable ownership, and reviewable surfaces.
- Transparency and interoperability can improve inspection without proving model performance.
- Lifecycle monitoring and bounded change control are useful governance patterns, especially in regulated device-software contexts.
- Vendor evidence and resilience checks belong inside AI assurance, not outside it.

## Claims to avoid

- Do not claim that AI broadly improves patient outcomes or lowers costs from this source set.
- Do not treat transparency metadata, framework adoption, contract language, or assurance guidance as proof of safety, compliance, maturity, or ROI.
- Do not generalize FDA device-software guidance to every workflow AI tool.
- Do not treat physician usage signals as evidence of audited deployment quality.

## Recommended structure for the report draft

1. Lead with the shift from adoption talk to operational proof.
2. Organize the body around four proof layers: reviewable surfaces, assurance operations, change discipline, and vendor resilience.
3. Close on human accountability and privacy boundaries as the conditions that keep healthcare AI governable.

## Source gaps

- No direct clinical outcome dataset.
- No private vendor diligence or implementation audit.
- No evidence of local compliance or readiness.
- No comprehensive market, funding, litigation, or M&A map.
