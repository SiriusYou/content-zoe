# W29 Research Brief: AI in Healthcare

## Core angle

Healthcare AI is no longer best framed as a speculative future story. The staged evidence supports a narrower claim: physician exposure is now common enough that governance, training, procurement, cybersecurity, and oversight are practical operating issues.

## What the evidence can support

- AMA survey data supports a strong workflow-adoption signal: reported physician AI use rose to 81 percent in 2026 from 38 percent in 2023, and average use cases per physician increased from 1.1 to 2.3. Common uses include research summarization and clinical documentation.
- FDA and HHS materials support an oversight narrative, not a single-rule narrative: lifecycle controls, predetermined change control, transparency, cybersecurity resilience, interoperability, and cross-agency coordination all matter.
- HSCC material supports a third-party risk and procurement narrative: AI governance now reaches contracts, data rights, training terms, performance standards, and vendor accountability.
- OECD material supports a scale-readiness narrative: health AI remains constrained by fragmented data, uneven policy readiness, workforce capacity, oversight gaps, and public-trust requirements.
- Privacy and safety references support reader boundaries: HIPAA has scope limits, de-identification claims need method and governance context, and WHO/NIST-style risk management is relevant but not proof of safe deployment.

## Recommended framing for the report

- Lead with operational maturity with guardrails, not breakthrough outcomes.
- Treat physician adoption as evidence of workflow exposure and organizational pressure to govern AI use.
- Separate three categories throughout the piece: regulated AI-enabled medical-device software, certified health IT, and broader workflow or administrative AI.
- Tie cybersecurity and third-party risk to real operating decisions such as procurement, contracting, monitoring, and accountability.
- Use OECD to widen the lens on scaling constraints, but do not present it as a U.S.-specific deployment metric.

## Claims to make carefully

- Safe: AI use is increasingly visible in physician work and health-system operations.
- Safe: Federal sources emphasize safe, secure, interoperable, and coordinated AI use.
- Safe: Governance, workforce training, procurement controls, and vendor-risk management remain central constraints on responsible scale-up.
- Safe: Third-party AI increases dependency and oversight demands even when a tool is not a regulated medical device.

## Claims to avoid

- Do not claim broad patient-outcome improvement.
- Do not imply universal health-system deployment or operational maturity.
- Do not treat survey self-reporting as audited production deployment.
- Do not imply FDA policy activity, HIPAA compliance, or de-identification language alone proves safety.
- Do not present vendor claims as independently validated.

## Suggested outline

1. Mainstream exposure: physician use is rising fast enough that health systems have to operationalize governance.
2. Oversight reality: FDA and HHS signals show healthcare AI is governed through lifecycle, cybersecurity, interoperability, and coordination layers.
3. Operational bottleneck: third-party AI risk shifts the conversation from principles to procurement, contracts, and accountability.
4. Scaling constraint: OECD shows the hard part is responsible scale, not proving AI is relevant.
5. Reader caution: privacy, safety, and validation limits remain visible throughout the piece.

## Source gaps

- No staged source validates specific vendors, products, or health systems.
- No staged source supports causal claims about clinical effectiveness or patient outcomes.
- No staged source provides a comprehensive market map or patient-level evidence.
