# Research Brief

## Core angle

This week's report should frame healthcare AI as an evidence-discipline story, not a novelty story. The local source set supports three linked observations: physician use is now broad enough to create operational pressure, federal interoperability and health IT governance work are making AI deployment more plausible, and credibility now depends on whether organizations can evaluate tools, govern vendors, and distinguish regulated-device evidence from ordinary workflow claims.

## Strongest supported claims

- AMA survey material supports an adoption signal: physician use is broad, and training, workflow fit, and human accountability remain active concerns.
- HHS and ONC material supports an infrastructure signal: interoperability, TEFCA-scale exchange, and algorithm-transparency policy work are making health AI more operationally relevant and more governable.
- FDA device materials support a regulatory signal: AI-enabled device software should be discussed in lifecycle-management and real-world-monitoring terms, not as if clearance alone settles performance questions.
- HSCC and FDA cybersecurity materials support an operational-risk signal: third-party AI creates procurement, supply-chain, accountability, and resilience risks even when the tool is not a regulated medical device.
- OECD, WHO, HHS, and NIST references support a boundary signal: privacy, de-identification, transparency, documentation, and risk management are continuing constraints, not solved prerequisites.

## Recommended narrative spine

1. Open with the shift from experimentation to operational exposure: healthcare no longer needs to decide whether it will encounter AI; it needs to decide how to govern it.
2. Use physician adoption as the pressure indicator, but immediately qualify it as self-reported usage rather than audited proof of clinical benefit.
3. Show why deployment is becoming easier: interoperability, TEFCA, and federal health IT governance create enabling conditions.
4. Pivot to the credibility test: regulated-device evidence, real-world monitoring, vendor accountability, and procurement discipline.
5. Close on the governing distinction: infrastructure progress and rising use make better governance more urgent, but they do not validate outcomes.

## Safe language and framing

- Prefer: "operational pressure," "enabling infrastructure," "evidence discipline," "governance maturity," and "real-world monitoring."
- Keep "augmented intelligence" framing when discussing physician workflows so the report centers support and accountability rather than replacement narratives.
- Separate regulated AI-enabled medical devices from non-device workflow, administrative, documentation, and consumer-facing AI.
- Treat privacy, de-identification, and general AI risk frameworks as boundary-setting references, not as proof that specific products are compliant or safe.

## Avoid these overclaims

- Do not claim that healthcare AI has broadly improved patient outcomes.
- Do not treat self-reported physician use as audited production deployment quality.
- Do not imply that interoperability progress or TEFCA scale proves clinical safety, fairness, or effectiveness.
- Do not imply that FDA device guidance validates non-device workflow AI.
- Do not imply that publication of vendor-risk or cybersecurity guidance means providers or vendors have implemented those controls.
- Do not treat HIPAA, de-identification, OECD, WHO, or NIST materials as product-level validation evidence.

## Evidence gaps to keep visible

- No local source provides audited provider deployment data, outcome studies, or vendor-by-vendor performance comparisons.
- No local source supports claims about broad ROI, market share, M&A, or funding momentum.
- No local source supports country-by-country readiness comparisons or uniform implementation maturity.
- If the draft needs stronger clinical-effectiveness claims, additional sources will be required outside this attempt-local bundle.
