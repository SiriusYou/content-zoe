# Healthcare AI Should Re-Earn Continued Use

Healthcare AI should not move from one renewal cycle to the next on the strength of launch approval alone.
Renewal is a separate governance checkpoint.
It asks whether the workflow still fits the purpose, users, setting, data, and downstream effects originally reviewed.

That distinction matters because continued use is a decision, not a default.
A tool can keep the same name while its model, interface, dependencies, support path, workflow, or operational burden changes underneath it.
The staged sources support disciplined review questions at that checkpoint.
They do not support claims that any named product, vendor, hospital, or health system is safe, effective, compliant, or worth renewing.

## Why Renewal Is Different From Launch Approval

Launch approval is usually based on planned use.
Renewal should examine actual use.
That means asking whether the tool is still being used for the reviewed purpose, in the reviewed setting, by the reviewed users, with the reviewed data, and with the expected downstream effects.

Launch review can approve a scoped workflow with training plans, fallback plans, monitoring plans, and ownership assignments.
Renewal asks what happened when those plans met real work.
Were overrides common.
Did handoffs become more fragile.
Did workflow burden rise.
Did downtime, complaints, or unresolved concerns change the local risk picture.

This is a governance argument, not a proof claim.
The staged material supports renewal questions and decision checkpoints.
It does not prove that renewal review itself produces safety, compliance, fairness, trust, or better outcomes.

## What Continued Use Should Recheck

Renewal should restate the approved use before judging continued use.
That baseline should include the approved purpose, expected users, expected setting, expected data, and anticipated downstream effects.

It should then compare that baseline with actual use in operation.
Relevant questions include who actually used the tool, where it was used, which queues or handoffs it affected, how often people overrode it, what complaints or incident reviews surfaced, and whether the workflow added burden rather than removing it.

Monitoring should also be reviewed against the approved-use assumptions.
The question is not only whether outputs looked acceptable.
The question is whether actual use still matches the boundaries reviewed at launch.
If the monitoring record does not show that comparison, the gap should remain visible in the renewal file.

Evidence gaps matter here.
If local logs, performance measures, complaint records, incident records, or measured outcomes are missing, that absence should shape the decision record rather than being silently ignored.

## What Changes Since Launch Can Reopen Review

A workflow may need renewed review even when the visible tool name is unchanged.
Material change can arrive through model or feature updates, interface redesigns, data-feed changes, EHR integration changes, identity or permission changes, staffing changes, support-model changes, or protocol changes around the tool.

Dependency shifts matter because they can change risk without changing the front-end label.
A new integration, a different escalation path, weaker support coverage, or an altered handoff can affect continued use even if the model itself was not replaced.

Training state and fallback readiness also need to be rechecked.
Renewal should ask whether current users still have the right training, whether fallback paths still work, whether ownership is still clear, and whether unresolved support issues or downtime concerns changed the operational picture.

## Vendor Evidence And Contract Checkpoints

Renewal is also the moment to ask what changed on the vendor side.
That can include model or feature change notices, updated evidence, support commitments, incident-notice expectations, audit access, and data return or deletion expectations.

These are governance inputs, not substitutes for local review.
Vendor assurances can help define the questions an organization should ask.
They do not replace local monitoring, local validation, workflow review, or accountability for actual use.

This section should remain non-legal.
The staged sources support asking about contract checkpoints, service history, notice practices, exit information, and responsibility after updates.
They do not provide local contract terms, legal conclusions, or a basis for ranking vendors or products.

## Decision Outcomes Should Stay Explicit

Renewal should end with a named decision rather than passive continuation.
Reasonable outcomes include continue, narrow scope, pause, reopen validation, replace, retire, or prepare transition.

If continued use is approved, the record should say whether approval is full-scope or narrowed.
If continued use is paused, replaced, or retired, the record should identify transition, communication, support, data, and fallback needs.

The decision record should also name the evidence considered, the unresolved gaps, the accountable owner, the next review date, and the triggers for an earlier review.
That keeps renewal reversible and helps prevent default lock-in.

## Exit Readiness Matters

Exit readiness does not prove resilience.
It does make continued use more reviewable because it shows the organization is not renewing only because switching is too hard.

An AI-enabled workflow is easier to govern when leaders know what would happen if the workflow had to narrow, pause, move to a fallback path, or retire.
That includes transition planning, support expectations, data-handling expectations, and continuity planning for affected staff and patients.
