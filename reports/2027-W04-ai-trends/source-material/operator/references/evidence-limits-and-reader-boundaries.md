# Evidence limits and reader boundaries

## Evidence posture

The source basis supports a cautious, governance-oriented article about disagreement paths for healthcare AI use. It can explain why human oversight, decision-support visibility, documentation, monitoring, system management, escalation, contingency planning, responsible adoption, and bounded device context make contestability important when an AI-enabled output is wrong, incomplete, uncertain, or hard to act on.

The source basis does not prove that any named organization has a disagreement process. It does not supply local dispute logs, override rates, owner rosters, committee charters, audit trails, utilization data, model-performance measurements, safety outcomes, equity outcomes, legal conclusions, or named implementation proof.

The article should therefore frame disagreement paths as a routing and governance discipline, not as proof that an AI-enabled workflow is safe, compliant, effective, fair, trusted, resilient, mature, or outcome-improving.

## Required warning posture

Use explicit evidence-limit warnings in both locales. The warnings should say that the article is limited to source-bounded governance, health IT, decision-support, safety-management, responsible-adoption, and bounded device-context material. They should also state that the article does not include local dispute logs, override rates, committee charters, audit logs, legal analysis, measured outcomes, or named implementation proof.

Warning language must be locale-pure. English comments should contain English only. Chinese comments should contain Chinese only.

Warnings should not describe how evidence was prepared. Keep the warning focused on what the source basis can and cannot prove.

## Reader questions the article may answer

- Why does a healthcare AI workflow need a visible way to challenge or question an output?
- What can go wrong if a user cannot document disagreement or route a concern?
- What fields make a disagreement path operationally useful?
- How do alternate actions, escalation contacts, monitoring, and review triggers fit together?
- Why should unknown, unusable, or disputed challenge routes remain visible?
- What can the available governance sources support, and what can they not prove?

## Reader questions the article must not answer

- Which vendor, product, hospital, health system, regulator, or public program has the best disagreement process?
- Whether a local challenge route satisfies law, regulation, accreditation, certification, procurement, privacy, civil rights, informed-consent, or professional-liability obligations.
- Whether a specific AI-enabled workflow is safe, compliant, effective, fair, locally validated, trusted, resilient, mature, or outcome-improving.
- Whether FDA device context applies to every healthcare AI workflow.
- Whether a specific local output should be accepted, overridden, escalated, or used in clinical care.

## Phrasing discipline

Use public-facing terms such as "the source basis", "available governance sources", "disagreement path", "challenge route", "alternate action", "escalation route", "review trigger", "use boundary", and "evidence gap".

Avoid language that points to non-reader operational details. The reader should see a healthcare AI governance article.

For `Selected Source Basis`, list public source families by name. Acceptable examples include NIST AI RMF, ONC SAFER guides, ONC decision-support intervention material, WHO, AMA, Joint Commission/CHAI, and bounded FDA AI/ML device context.
