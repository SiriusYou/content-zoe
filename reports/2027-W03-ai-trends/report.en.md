# Healthcare AI Should Carry a Review Date

A healthcare AI workflow can look settled once it has been approved, launched, or assigned to an owner. That appearance can be misleading. A workflow may keep the same name even as its users, setting, evidence, inputs, outputs, or downstream decisions change.

That is why healthcare AI use should carry a review date, not just a launch date. A review date makes continued-use questions visible.

The available source basis supports a governance-oriented argument for time-bounded review. It does not support the claim that a review date proves safety, compliance, effectiveness, fairness, resilience, maturity, or improved outcomes.

That narrower point still matters. It keeps attention on whether the earlier decision still fits the current use. Many healthcare organizations know how to mark a workflow as approved, but they may be less explicit about how that approval stays current over time.

Evidence can age even when no one announces a major change. Vendor material can change, and local reliance on that material can change as well.

The workflow can also move into a different setting or reach a different user group. Its output may begin shaping a different downstream decision than before.

Monitoring expectations can drift after launch. Signals that once seemed sufficient may no longer match the actual use boundary.

Fallback readiness can drift in the same way. A process that once looked workable may no longer fit current operations.

Ownership can become blurred, shared, or disputed over time. That matters because a date without someone able to act on it is only a label.

A review date gives continued use a visible checkpoint instead of letting old approval language stand in for a current decision. It creates a recurring moment to ask whether the prior decision still matches the present workflow, not just the original description.

That is why the review date should attach to workflow use rather than to a model name, procurement event, or software version alone.

Useful review-date fields can include the current owner, the reviewed use boundary, the last review date, and the next review date.

Useful fields can also include trigger categories, monitoring responsibility, fallback contact, escalation route, unresolved evidence gaps, and, when available, a continued-use decision.

The value of the record is not that it looks complete. The value is that it helps route action.

Trigger categories matter because they reopen review when conditions change. They do not need to prove harm or noncompliance before they become relevant.

Examples can include a changed setting, changed intended users, changed inputs, changed output use, updated vendor material, new monitoring concerns, changed fallback readiness, a changed escalation route, or an unresolved evidence gap reaching a decision point.

The goal is not to publish a universal checklist for every healthcare setting. The goal is to keep continued-use review sensitive to drift after launch.

Available governance sources can support lifecycle and review-trigger questions. They can support asking whether the current use boundary still matches the last reviewed one.

They can also support asking whether the people who monitor the workflow, respond to concerns, and escalate unresolved issues are still the right ones.

This makes the review date a routing device rather than a verdict. It points the organization toward a decision instead of pretending the date itself is the decision.

That decision may be to continue within the reviewed use boundary. It may also be to continue with conditions, gather evidence, narrow use, revise fallback, escalate, pause, retire, or reopen governance review.

Unknown or expired review status should remain visible. Past approval is not the same as current justification.

An expired review date should not quietly turn into assumed approval. Unknown status should not be treated as if someone else already resolved it.

Disputed status should remain visible as well. It should route attention to a decision-maker instead of disappearing behind a generic label.

Monitoring, fallback, and escalation belong near the review discussion for the same reason. They make continued-use questions more actionable when the workflow has changed.

Still, the article should stay cautious about what those fields can prove. Monitoring responsibility does not prove that the workflow is safe or effective.

Fallback planning does not prove resilience or continuity. An escalation route does not satisfy every local governance obligation.

The source basis can support governance, lifecycle review, monitoring, contingency planning, decision-support visibility, responsible adoption, and bounded device-context discussion. It does not provide local review calendars, committee charters, audit logs, legal analysis, measured outcomes, or named implementation proof.

That boundary matters because the review-date argument is not a claim about any named vendor, product, hospital, health system, or regulator.

It is also not a claim that bounded FDA AI/ML device context describes the whole healthcare AI landscape.

The argument is simpler: healthcare AI use can change after launch, and continued use deserves a visible moment of reconsideration.

That moment should ask whether the old decision still fits the current use boundary, current evidence, current monitoring signals, current fallback readiness, and current ownership.

If the answer is unclear, the uncertainty should remain visible too. That is the discipline a review date can introduce.
