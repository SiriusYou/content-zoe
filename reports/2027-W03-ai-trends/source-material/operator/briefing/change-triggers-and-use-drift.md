# Change triggers and use drift

## Why review should not rely only on the original approval moment

Healthcare AI use can change after the initial decision. A use that once appeared narrow can expand to new units, users, patient groups, operational settings, or downstream decisions. Evidence can become stale. Vendor materials can change. Monitoring expectations can shift. A fallback plan can become outdated. Staffing and ownership can change.

The review date should make these changes visible. It should not assume that the original review remains accurate just because the workflow still carries the same name.

## Trigger categories the article may discuss

- expanded clinical or operational setting;
- changed intended users;
- changed input data, workflow step, or output use;
- model, software, or vendor-material update;
- new monitoring signal, complaint, near miss, or incident;
- fairness, usability, or workflow-fit concern;
- fallback plan no longer matching current operations;
- owner, committee, or escalation route changing;
- retirement, replacement, or archive planning;
- unresolved evidence gap reaching a decision point.

These are governance trigger categories, not automatic legal conclusions. A trigger can reopen review without proving harm or noncompliance.

## Important boundary

Do not present any trigger list as complete or mandatory for every setting. Different organizations, workflows, risk contexts, regulatory contexts, and professional responsibilities may require different review processes. The source basis supports the idea that lifecycle review should remain attentive to change; it does not supply a universal local trigger checklist.
