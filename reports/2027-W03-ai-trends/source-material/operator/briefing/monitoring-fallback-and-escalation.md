# Monitoring, fallback, and escalation

## Why monitoring belongs near the review date

Monitoring signals can change the meaning of continued use. A review date should not be detached from who watches the workflow, what signals are available, what concerns have surfaced, and who can respond. Monitoring responsibility can make the review record more actionable, but it does not prove that the workflow is safe or effective.

The article may connect monitoring to lifecycle governance: review dates create a recurring moment to ask whether monitoring remains adequate for the current use boundary and whether unresolved signals need escalation.

## Fallback and continuity questions

Healthcare AI workflows may require a practical fallback path when the tool is unavailable, unreliable, paused, retired, or no longer fits the workflow. A review date can ask whether the fallback contact and fallback process still match current operations.

The article should stay cautious. Fallback planning can improve reviewability and operational clarity, but it does not prove resilience, safety, continuity, or patient trust.

## Escalation route

A review date is weaker if no one knows where unresolved questions go. Escalation routes are useful because they connect stale evidence, disputed ownership, monitoring concerns, or changed use to a decision-maker.

The article may describe escalation as a routing function. It must not imply that escalation alone resolves the issue or satisfies a legal, clinical, operational, or accreditation requirement.
